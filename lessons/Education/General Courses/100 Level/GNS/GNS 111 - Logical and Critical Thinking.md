## What is critical thinking?

Critical thinking is the careful, disciplined analysis of information, claims and arguments before accepting or rejecting them. It means asking: *What is being claimed? What is the evidence? Is the reasoning sound?* It is not the same as criticising or finding fault.

**Why it matters:** in your studies, in writing and speaking, and when judging news, social media posts and advertisements.

## Arguments

An **argument** is a set of statements in which some (the **premises**) are offered as reasons to accept another (the **conclusion**).

- *All students must register. Ada is a student. **Therefore**, Ada must register.*
  - Premises: all students must register; Ada is a student.
  - Conclusion: Ada must register.
- Indicator words: **conclusion** - therefore, so, thus, hence, it follows that. **Premise** - because, since, for, given that.

## Deductive and inductive reasoning

| | Deductive | Inductive |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | general to specific | specific to general |
| If premises are true | conclusion **must** be true | conclusion is **probably** true |
| Example | All humans are mortal. Ngozi is human. So Ngozi is mortal. | Every swan I have seen is white. So all swans are probably white. |

- A deductive argument is **valid** if the conclusion follows from the premises, and **sound** if it is valid **and** the premises are true.
- An inductive argument is **strong** or **weak**, depending on how well the evidence supports the conclusion.

## Common fallacies (errors in reasoning)

| Fallacy | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ad hominem** | attacks the person, not the argument | "Don't listen to him, he failed Maths." |
| **Appeal to authority** | uses an irrelevant or unnamed authority | "A famous footballer says this drink is healthy." |
| **Bandwagon** | "everyone does it, so it is right" | "Everybody cheats in exams, so it is fine." |
| **Hasty generalisation** | concludes from too little evidence | "Two lecturers were late, so all lecturers are lazy." |
| **False dilemma** | offers only two options when more exist | "Either you support me or you are against me." |
| **Straw man** | misrepresents an argument to attack it | "She wants stricter rules, so she wants to ban all fun." |
| **Circular reasoning** | the conclusion is already assumed in the premise | "He is honest because he never lies." |
| **Red herring** | distracts with an irrelevant point | "Why worry about exams when there is hunger in the world?" |
| **Slippery slope** | claims one step will inevitably lead to extremes | "If we allow phones in class, students will stop learning." |
| **Post hoc** | assumes that because B followed A, A caused B | "I wore my lucky shirt and passed, so the shirt made me pass." |

## Steps for thinking critically

1. **Identify** the claim or conclusion.
2. **Find** the reasons and evidence offered.
3. **Check the source** - who says this, and do they have expertise or a motive?
4. **Test the reasoning** - does the conclusion follow? Any fallacy?
5. **Consider alternatives** - other explanations or viewpoints.
6. **Decide** - accept, reject or suspend judgement, and be able to explain why.

## Presenting a clear argument

A meaningful presentation has: a **clear position** (thesis), **logically ordered reasons**, **supporting evidence** (facts, examples, statistics, expert sources), a **response to counter-arguments**, and a **conclusion** that follows from the reasons.

## Practice

Name the fallacy:
1. "You can't trust her argument on fees; she dresses badly." (Answer: ad hominem)
2. "My cousin got sick after the vaccine, so the vaccine is dangerous." (Answer: post hoc / hasty generalisation)
3. "Either we ban phones completely or students will never learn." (Answer: false dilemma)
