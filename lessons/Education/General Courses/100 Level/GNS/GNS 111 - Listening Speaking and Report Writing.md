## The four language skills

| | Receptive (taking in) | Productive (giving out) |
|---|---|---|
| **Spoken** | Listening | Speaking |
| **Written** | Reading | Writing |

Effective communication needs all four. Listening and speaking are the **oral skills**; reading and writing are the **literacy skills.**

## Listening

Hearing is physical; **listening** is understanding and responding to what is said.

**Types of listening**
- **Appreciative** - for enjoyment (music, poetry).
- **Informational** - to learn (lectures).
- **Critical** - to judge an argument or claim.
- **Empathic** - to understand another person's feelings.

**Barriers to listening:** noise, distraction, tiredness, prejudice, assuming you already know, preparing your reply while the other person is speaking, poor speaker delivery.

**Active listening tips**
1. Give full attention; put your phone away.
2. Look at the speaker; use appropriate body language.
3. Do not interrupt.
4. Take short notes of key points.
5. Ask clarifying questions; paraphrase to check understanding.

## Public speaking

Public speaking is delivering a prepared message to an audience.

**Preparing**
1. **Know your purpose** - to inform, persuade, entertain or mark an occasion.
2. **Know your audience** - age, background, expectations.
3. **Research and organise** your content.
4. **Practise** aloud, and time yourself.

**Structure**
- **Introduction** - gain attention (a question, story or fact), state your topic, preview your points.
- **Body** - two to four main points, each with evidence or examples; use clear transitions (first, next, however, finally).
- **Conclusion** - summarise the main points and end with a memorable closing line.

**Delivery**
- **Voice:** speak clearly, vary pitch and pace, pause for emphasis, use enough volume.
- **Body language:** stand steadily, make eye contact, use natural gestures, avoid distracting habits.
- **Language:** simple, correct and suited to the audience.
- **Visuals:** keep slides short and readable; do not read from them.

**Managing nervousness:** prepare well, breathe slowly, start with a strong opening you know by heart, focus on your message not yourself, and practise in front of friends.

## Report writing

A **report** presents facts and findings about a situation, event or investigation, usually for a particular reader who needs to act on it. It should be accurate, clear, objective and well organised.

**Typical structure of a formal report**
1. **Title** (and, for long reports, a title page)
2. **Terms of reference / introduction** - purpose and scope
3. **Methodology** - how the information was gathered (observation, interviews, questionnaires)
4. **Findings** - the facts discovered, in logical order
5. **Conclusion** - what the findings mean
6. **Recommendations** - what should be done
7. **Signature, name, date**

**Types of report:** incident or accident report, committee report, minutes of a meeting, progress report, research or project report, event report.

**Report style**
- Use **formal, impersonal** language; avoid slang and contractions.
- Use headings and numbered points for clarity.
- Be objective: separate facts from opinions.
- Be concise, accurate and consistent in tense (usually the past tense for what happened).

**Minutes of a meeting** record: name of the organisation, date, time and venue; attendance and absentees; agenda items discussed; decisions and who is responsible for action; time of adjournment; signature of the secretary and chairman.
