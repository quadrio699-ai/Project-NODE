## Chapter 7: Curriculum Process Models

- Nicholls and Nicholls (1972): the curriculum process is a continuous **cycle** of activities relating the elements and their interrelatedness.
- All experts agree curriculum has elements related to each other. Some see the relationship as **linear/sequential**, others as **cyclical**.

### Tyler's model (1949) - "the objective model" or "rational model"

- Prescribes a rational model emphasising **goal attainment**. Answers his four fundamental questions.
- A clear statement of purpose comes from analysing three **goal sources: the society, the subject matter and the student.**
- Tyler strongly favours **behaviourally stated objectives**. Content, method and evaluation are chosen to reflect them.
- Steps: **1. Objectives** (screened through the learner, contemporary life, subject specialists, philosophy and psychology) -> **2. Selection of learning experiences** -> **3. Organisation of learning experiences** -> **4. Evaluation.**
- *Criticisms:* too much emphasis on objectives (goal-oriented); phases need not be sequential; curriculum development can start from any point, not only the first phase.

### Bruner (1960) - a cyclical view

- Evaluation should not wait until the last stage. Evaluation helps determine content, and content helps determine objectives.
- Methods help determine evaluation strategies, and evaluation results can be used to modify methods, content or objectives.
- Curriculum is an entity that keeps evolving; all elements form a dynamic, continuous, organic whole.

### Taba (1964) - seven linear steps

1. **Diagnosis of needs** - survey the situation to see if a curriculum is needed.
2. **Formulation of objectives.**
3. **Selection of content** - choosing the subject matter.
4. **Organisation of content.**
5. **Selection of learning experiences.**
6. **Organisation of learning experiences.**
7. **Determination of what to evaluate, and the ways and means of doing it.**

### Nicholls and Nicholls (1972) - cyclical model

Four main elements: (1) selection of objectives, (2) selection and organisation of content, (3) selection and organisation of method, (4) assessment and evaluation. They added a very important phase: **situation analysis** - the teacher studies the pupils' home background, school climate, facilities and equipment. Their revised process says curriculum development is **continuous and dynamic**, not done once.

### Wheeler (1967) - "process model" / cyclical model

Wrote the book *Curriculum Process*. Five phases:

1. Selection of aims, goals and objectives
2. Selection of content (subject matter through which experiences are offered)
3. Selection of learning experiences
4. Organisation and integration of learning experiences and content
5. Evaluation of the effectiveness of all aspects in attaining the goals

The difference between Wheeler and Nicholls and Nicholls is mainly the terminology. Critics see the numbering of the stages as a weakness.

## Chapter 8: Participants in Curriculum Development

1. Learners
2. Teachers
3. Educational administrators
4. Publishers
5. Curriculum planners / evaluators
6. Examination bodies
7. Accreditation bodies
8. Society / public

- **Planners / evaluators** research learning activities and instructional strategies. In Nigeria they come from **NERDC** (Nigerian Educational Research and Development Council), **WAEC** (West African Examinations Council) and ministries of education.
- **Administrators** have the final say; they give the "go-ahead" before implementation. They sit in local, state and federal ministries of education.
- **Teachers** are the arbitrators, interpreters and operators who turn theory into practice. They include subject specialists, librarians and counsellors. Their values and view of the school's goals act as the filter through which educational purposes are applied (Saylor and Alexander, 1981).
- The main purpose of curriculum development is **to change the learner's behaviour.** Students also take part in the "hidden curriculum". Agreement between teachers' and students' goals is a good basis for an effective curriculum.
- **Examination bodies** are involved because of Nigeria's emphasis on certification: WAEC (Lagos), NTI - National Teachers' Institute (Kaduna), etc.
- **Society** must be involved in what is taught and how.
- **Publishers** matter because in many schools the textbook is the curriculum, and published materials strongly influence it.
- **Accreditation bodies** accredit courses and set minimum standards: **NUC** (universities), **NBTE** (polytechnics and colleges of technology), **NCCE** (colleges of education and advanced teachers' colleges). NUC's accreditation role was criticised; ASUU backed this with a seven-week strike that ended with an agreement with the Federal Government in August 1992.

### A good curriculum must reflect

1. Societal goals, objectives, needs and aspirations
2. The national outlook, including local values and interests
3. Good planning and organisation that integrates individual learners into their environment
4. Continuous evaluation to give feedback for modification
5. Comprehensiveness and realism
6. Cognitive, affective and psychomotor development of learners

## Chapter 9: Philosophy and Curriculum

- A society's norms, culture and value system determine the form of its education. The aims, methods, content and evaluation of any curriculum depend on its **philosophy of education.**
- **Philosophy** comes from the Greek *philo* (love of) and *sophia* (wisdom). It examines the fundamental assumptions of other branches of knowledge.
- Three main branches: **Metaphysics** (the nature of reality), **Epistemology** (the nature and structure of knowledge), **Axiology** (the theory of values: worth and appreciation).
- **Phenix (1965):** philosophy of education is a coherent set of values and fundamental assumptions used to evaluate and guide educational practice.
- **Akinpelu (1984):** the theoretical and general conceptual basis of current educational practice.
- **Kneller (1971):** we cannot criticise or propose educational policy without considering: the nature of the good life that education should lead to; the nature of man (education is a social process); the nature of ultimate reality.

### Three schools of philosophic thought

**Idealism**
- Man is a spiritual being; reality is spiritual; the mental, not the material, is emphasised. Physical objects exist but are manifestations of ultimate reality.
- Began with Socrates; made popular by his student **Plato (427-347 BC)**, who held that everything comes from reason and ideas endure. Others: Spinoza (1632-1677), Descartes (1596-1650), Kant (1724-1804), Hegel (1770-1831).
- Knowledge gives meaning and order to what our senses gather; truth depends on how comprehensive and consistent the system of ideas is (the **coherence theory of truth**).
- Assumptions: good, truth and beauty are constant and found through reason; the mind is the only thing truly real; true ideas, values and knowledge do not change.

**Realism**
- Began with **Aristotle**, who saw God as the prime mover or first cause. Others: Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274), John Locke (1632-1704), Herbart (1776-1841), William James (1842-1910).
- Matter exists outside our minds; the world around us and change are real, but change follows permanent laws of nature.
- Assumptions: things exist independently of our opinion; nature is governed by laws we cannot control; body and mind exist together, with equal importance.
- Idealists are subjective; realists are objective.

**Pragmatism**
- From the Greek *pragma*, meaning "work". Concerned with what works.
- Traced to Heraclitus' emphasis on change (against Plato's permanence); the modern school is American. Others: Francis Bacon (1561-1626), Auguste Comte (1798-1857), Charles Sanders Peirce, John Dewey (1859-1952).
- Themes: the reality of change; the social and biological nature of man; the relativity of values; the use of critical intelligence.
- Assumptions: all things are continuously changing; what is true or good must work and meet our needs; we know only through experience, and new experience can change old learning; curriculum content is dynamic, not fixed; learning is child-centred, not subject-centred; the teacher does not simply pour knowledge into pupils; teaching and learning should be empirical.

## Chapter 10: Contemporary Educational Theories

**Perennialism** - belief in absolute principles; what is real is what is permanent. Assumptions: human nature is the same everywhere; reason is man's highest attribute and should direct his instincts; education imparts eternal truths; education is a preparation for life, not an imitation of it; students should study great works of literature, philosophy, history, government and science.

**Progressivism** - formalised by John Dewey and W. H. Kilpatrick, building on Francis W. Parker (1870s). Education is the continual reconstruction of experience. Assumptions: education is life itself, not preparation for living; learning relates to the child's interest; problem solving comes before inculcating subject matter; the teacher advises rather than directs; the school encourages co-operation over competition; only democracy allows the free interplay of ideas needed for growth.

**Essentialism** - founded in the early 1930s (Bagley, Briggs, Breed, Kandel); draws on both perennialism and progressivism. Aims: re-examine the curriculum, separate the essential from the non-essential, and re-establish the teacher's authority. Assumptions: learning involves hard work and often unwilling effort; the teacher, not the pupil, takes the initiative; the heart of education is mastering the prescribed subject matter; traditional mental discipline stays.

**Existentialism** - listed in the compendium but not explained. In brief (supplementary): it stresses individual freedom, choice and responsibility, and says each person creates his own meaning.

### Traditionalists and progressives (two extremes)

**Traditionalists** (perennialists and essentialists): curriculum is a body of cultural heritage or accumulated knowledge placed in subjects and passed from teachers to learners. The school's main purpose is mental discipline through permanent studies. Perennialists include grammar, reading, mathematics, rhetoric and logic (mathematics for clear thinking, grammar for logical faculty). Essentialists (Onwuka, 1984) name five areas: grammar, literature and writing, mathematics, sciences, history and foreign languages.
Features: accumulated knowledge in compartments; subject-centred; fixed content prepared by the teacher (teacher-centred); preparation for adulthood; academic studies with intrinsic value.

**Progressives:** curriculum is more than passing on heritage or intellectual training. No knowledge has intrinsic value; its worth is its usefulness.
Features: stress on needs and interests; a lot of problem solving (learning by doing); a democratic school; knowledge tested by experiment and held tentatively; practical outcomes; education that is socially relevant. School subjects include what used to be called extracurricular, co-curricular or hidden curriculum.

### More definitions of curriculum

- **Tyler (1949):** all the learning of students planned and directed by the school to attain its goals - more than subjects or syllabus.
- **Mackenzie (1964):** the learner's engagement with aspects of the environment planned under the school's auspices.
- **Inlow (1966):** all school experiences, like an umbrella.
- **Tanner and Tanner (1975):** planned and guided learning experiences and intended outcomes, formed by the systematic reconstruction of knowledge and experience under the school, for the learner's continuous and wilful growth in personal and social competence.

*Splitting definitions into "traditionalist" and "progressive" is just for convenience.*
