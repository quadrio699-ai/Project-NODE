## Chapter 1: Introduction

### Common misconceptions about "curriculum"

Curriculum means different things to different people. Non-experts often treat it as the same thing as:

- **School subject** - the prescribed content of a subject, e.g. "mathematics curriculum".
- **Syllabus** - the outline of a course that leads to certification, e.g. the WAEC syllabus.
- **Scheme of work** - the syllabus broken down for a particular class into units, based on the number of lessons on the timetable. It can cover one term (13 weeks) or a year (3 terms).
- **Timetable** - shows which subjects are taught on which days and for how long.
- **Lesson note** - the scheme of work narrowed to the actual learning activities for one period (e.g. 40 minutes). It is the teacher's step-by-step guide.
- **Course of study** - an educational programme designed for a specific purpose and period, e.g. the NCE course.

Curriculum is **broader than all of these**. It embraces all of them and more.

### The concept of curriculum

- The word comes from the Latin **currere**, meaning "a running course" - a course to be run before reaching a goal.
- **Tyler (1949):** all the learning of students that is planned and directed by the school to attain its educational goals. Tyler said a curriculum must answer four questions:
  1. What educational purpose should the school seek to attain?
  2. What educational experiences can be provided that are likely to attain these purposes?
  3. How can these experiences be effectively organised?
  4. How can we determine whether these purposes are being attained?
  - *Criticism:* too much emphasis on objectives. Critics say the four components should be equally important.
- **Saylor and Alexander (1974):** the total effort of the school to bring about desired outcomes in school and out-of-school situations.
- **Wheeler (1967):** the planned experiences offered to the learner under the guidance of the school.
- **Oliver (1977):** stresses learning experiences. Planned experiences have three elements:
  1. **Programme of studies** - transmission of knowledge in school subjects (academic programme).
  2. **Programme of activities** - learning outside the classroom: games, sports, clubs, societies.
  3. **Programme of guidance** - uses a learner's past and present to guide him into the future.
- **Inlow (1966):** curriculum covers all school experiences, like an umbrella.

## Chapter 2: Elements of Curriculum

The four fundamental elements:

1. **Objectives** - the purpose of the curriculum (why introduce it?). A statement of intended outcome.
2. **Content** - what should be presented to learners so they reach the desired outcomes (what is taught).
3. **Method** - how the content is presented.
4. **Evaluation** - determining whether the intended outcomes have been achieved.

**Tyler's view:** the relationship is linear and sequential: Objectives -> Content -> Method -> Evaluation.

**Nicholls (1972) and others:** in practice you do not move in a straight line. You keep moving backward and forward. When considering content you refer back to objectives and forward to methods; when considering methods you refer to content and objectives. This shows how closely the elements are related.

### The hidden curriculum

Many definitions stress only planned experiences. The **hidden curriculum** covers what a pupil learns in school that is **not on the timetable and not arranged by the teacher** (Ozigi and Canham, 1978). For example, things learnt from classmates or from watching the teacher's behaviour.

- Attitudes and values of the people who create arrangements are communicated to pupils accidentally (Kelly, 1982). A neat maths teacher can unconsciously pass on neatness; a strict teacher can instil fear.
- What is acquired can be **positive or negative**.
- Urevbu (1985): "non-academic" but educationally significant consequences of schooling.
- It is a by-product of planned learning, but the school should keep trying to control it, and the teacher should accept responsibility for its effects.
- It is the same as the **unplanned** or **implicit** curriculum. Dewey called it **collateral learning**.

### Other terms

- **Extracurricular / informal curriculum:** sports, clubs, societies, excursions.
- **Formal curriculum:** activities explicitly stated and taught at specific periods (the programme of studies).
- **Official / intended curriculum:** what is laid down in syllabi, prospectuses and schemes of work.
- **Actual curriculum:** what is really covered in school practice (Doll, 1964).
- The difference between them is **intention versus reality**. Formal curriculum is theory; actual curriculum is practice.

## Chapter 3: Aims, Goals and Objectives

These give direction to education and appear at different levels of planning. The main difference between them is **how specific they are**.

### Aim

- A broad, general statement reflecting a nation's or society's philosophy of education.
- The broadest outcome; cannot be measured and takes a long time to attain.
- Stated in the **National Policy on Education (NPE)**, first published in 1977 and revised later.
- The Second National Development Plan said Nigeria should build: a free and democratic society; a just and egalitarian society; a united, strong and self-reliant nation; a great and dynamic economy; a land of bright and full opportunities for all citizens.
- So the aims of Nigerian education are:
  1. the inculcation of national consciousness and national unity;
  2. the inculcation of the right values and attitudes for the survival of the individual and society;
  3. the training of the mind in the understanding of the world around;
  4. the acquisition of appropriate skills, abilities and competencies, mental and physical, to live in and contribute to society.
- Aims use vague words (appreciate, understand, know, inculcate), so they are too vague for classroom instruction. **Aims are broken down into goals.**

### Goals and objectives

- **Goals** are general intentions; **objectives** are specific desired changes.
- Goals suit curriculum planning; objectives suit instruction.
- There is no curriculum without goals and objectives. Kerr (1968): to be rational, a curriculum must start with clear aims, goals and objectives, and only then look for the content and methods to achieve them.

### Wheeler's three types of goals (1967)

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ultimate goal** | The final, highest outcome. Broad, hard to measure, achieved after a long time. | To produce a literate citizen |
| **Mediate goal** | The ultimate goal broken into smaller parts; behaviour to be reached at a particular stage. | Ability to read and write in the mother tongue (end of primary school) |
| **Proximate goal** | A more precise, limited part of a mediate goal; easy to observe; achieved in a short time. | Ability to read in the mother tongue (by Primary 3) |

Instructional / behavioural objectives are derived from proximate goals. So goals are "general objectives" and instructional objectives are "specific objectives".

*Note: the original compendium calls "to produce a literate" both an ultimate goal and a mediate goal in different places. The table above uses the consistent version.*

### Behavioural objectives

Objectives help the teacher plan instruction, guide students' learning and give criteria for evaluating outcomes. A **behavioural objective** states an outcome that can be observed or measured, in clear and specific terms.

A good objective has three parts:

1. **A learning product, not a learning process.** It uses an action verb showing what the student will do: mention, add, calculate, identify, construct, titrate, prepare, measure, demonstrate.
2. **The condition** under which the learner will show it (e.g. with paper and pencil, or with apparatus).
3. **The level of acceptable performance.**

Verbs like "understand" or "appreciate" are **not** behavioural because you cannot observe them (Lehmann and Mehrens, 1973).

**Common errors when writing objectives**

- Listing topics instead of objectives (e.g. "functions of money", "principles of taxation").
- Stating a learning process instead of a product (e.g. "punctuation of an English passage").
- Describing the **teacher's** behaviour instead of the student's (e.g. "to demonstrate the procedure for testing for starch").

**Functions of educational objectives**

1. They guide the choice of learning materials for teacher and students.
2. Knowing the expected outcomes guides the teacher's planning.
3. They guide the evaluation of curriculum achievement.
4. If clearly stated, another teacher can take over the lesson if the teacher is absent.

**Demerits of educational objectives**

1. They can limit the work of a creative teacher.
2. Some learning cannot easily be expressed in behavioural terms.
3. It is hard to state objectives behaviourally for synthesis, analysis, appreciation and creative thinking.
