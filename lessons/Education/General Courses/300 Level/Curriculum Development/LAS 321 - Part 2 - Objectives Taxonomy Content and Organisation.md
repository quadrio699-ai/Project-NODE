## Chapter 4: Sources of Educational Goals and Objectives

Three sources should be considered:

1. **Sociological / historical.** Societies make different demands on education. An agrarian (rural) society wants different things from an industrialised one. Goals should reflect the culture, norms, values and mores of the society, and the nation's philosophy, aspirations and ideology.
2. **Psychological.** The characteristics of the learner: age, maturation, ability (gifted, average, low), readiness, intelligence, self-concept. These fall under four headings: **physical, social, emotional and mental**. Also consider individual differences, needs and interests, and write a variety of objectives to cater for them.
3. **Nature of the discipline or subject matter.** Knowledge in each subject keeps growing, so objectives must reflect new knowledge. Science objectives use verbs such as draw, identify, calculate, titrate; humanities use describe, narrate, recite, compose.

## Taxonomy of Educational Objectives (Bloom, 1956)

Bloom placed all objectives in three domains. Categories in each domain go from **simple to complex**. The domains are not mutually exclusive; they show emphasis.

1. **Cognitive domain** - the head (knowledge and thinking)
2. **Affective domain** - the heart (feelings, attitudes, values)
3. **Psychomotor domain** - the hand (motor skills)

### 1. Cognitive domain (six categories, simplest to most complex)

| Category | Meaning | Typical verbs | Example objective |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge** | Remembering what was learnt: facts, words, symbols, principles, theories. Lowest level. | name, state, define, label, identify, describe | Define osmosis |
| **Comprehension** | Interpreting information and predicting consequences. | explain, generalise, predict, distinguish, infer, estimate, summarise | Explain the effects of demand and supply on price |
| **Application** | Using learnt information in new situations. | manipulate, modify, solve, relate, discover, demonstrate, complete | Solve a simple simultaneous equation |
| **Analysis** | Breaking material into parts and seeing the relationships between them. | separate, identify, distinguish, differentiate, classify, detect | Describe the process of separating a mixture of sand and water |
| **Synthesis** | Putting parts together to form a new whole; creativity. | design, compose, generate, devise, formulate, relate | Compose a tribute for fallen heroes |
| **Evaluation** | Judging the value of material against definite criteria. Highest and most complex. | compare, justify, criticise, discriminate | Criticise the role of the British in promoting disunity in pre-independence Nigeria |

### 2. Affective domain (five categories)

Emphasises interest, attitude, feelings, appreciation and adjustment. It is hard to state in precise terms.

| Category | Meaning | Typical verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Receiving** | Willingness to attend to something. Lowest level. | locate, select, reply, choose, ask | Choose appropriate words for a letter to a friend |
| **Responding** | Active participation. | assist, read, answer, report, practise, write, greet | Write a report on a visit to Kainji Dam |
| **Valuing** | Attaching value to something. | share, select, join, explain, report, form, initiate | Share with classmates their feelings on military involvement in politics |
| **Organisation** | Comparing, relating and synthesising values. | arrange, defend, compare, order, integrate, organise, relate, generalise | Defend the idea of returning schools to missionaries |
| **Characterisation by a value complex** | A consistent, predictable lifestyle; a philosophy of life. | solve, use, propose, discriminate, listen, revise, question | Question parents' involvement in their children's choice of career |

### 3. Psychomotor domain

Motor skills and manipulation: typing, operating machines, handwriting, swimming, laboratory skills. Verbs include build, construct, correct, calibrate, set, weigh, sketch, paint, locate, dismantle, design.

- Cognitive behaviours are a prerequisite for affective ones, and affective behaviours are prerequisites for psychomotor ones.
- Four ascending levels: **observing, imitating, practising, adapting.**

## Chapter 5: Curriculum Content and Organisation

- **Learning experience** (Tyler, 1949): the interaction between the learner and the external conditions in the environment to which he can react.
- **Onuoha et al. (1986):** the activities and opportunities created for the learner to achieve the changes stated in the objectives.
- **Content** is what makes up the course outline, subject unit or syllabus.
- **Wheeler (1967):** three types of learning experience - physical, mental and emotional. They are the basic classroom activities derived from content.

### Criteria for selecting content

1. **Validity** - Will the experience contribute to the desired outcome? Is the knowledge fundamental (key concepts, rules, facts, principles, theories)? Is it authentic and up to date, not outdated?
2. **Significance** - Useful and consequential, not trivial. It should meet the aspirations of the learner and society and prepare the learner to cope with a changing world.
3. **Needs and interests of the student** - Maslow (1954) identified primary needs (food, air, sleep), emotional needs (love, security) and social needs (acceptance by peers).
4. **Learner's ability** - Introduce new ideas, but do not teach what learners cannot learn. Link the present experience to the next, and consider readiness.
5. **Provision for a wide range of objectives** - Content should not be narrow; it should serve cognitive, affective and psychomotor objectives, and the teacher should consider good and bad side-effects.
6. **Durability (lasting quality)** - Relevant to present and future life, and useful for solving problems in the learner's environment.
7. **Cumulative** - New content builds on the previous and is the foundation for the next, from simple to complex.

### Selecting learning experiences (Doll, 1964: ten questions)

1. Can the pupils we teach benefit from the experience?
2. Does it meet the needs of our pupils (individual differences, learning styles, values)?
3. Are pupils likely to be interested?
4. Does it encourage pupils to inquire further?
5. Does it seem real (real objects to handle)?
6. Does it fit the life patterns, background and environment of pupils?
7. How contemporary is it?
8. How fundamental is it to mastery of the total content?
9. Does it serve a range of objectives?
10. Does it allow broad and deep study (not too detailed, not scanty)?

### Organising learning experiences

Content is the broad outline of topics; learning experiences are the detailed analysis of that content. Four organising problems:

- **Sequence** - the order of presentation. Concepts return at a more advanced level (Saylor and Alexander, 1981). Arrange from simple to complex, general to specific, or past to present. A prerequisite must come before what depends on it.
- **Continuity** - experiences should be related in two dimensions. **Horizontal:** relationship between subjects or periods on the same day. **Vertical:** how experiences flow across consecutive periods or classes (e.g. class one to class two).
- **Scope** - how much to include. Not too broad or diffuse, not too narrow; just enough to produce the stated outcomes.
- **Integration** - show that areas of knowledge are interrelated, rather than discrete (Hirst, 1965; Phenix, 1964). **Horizontal integration:** what is learnt in one subject is interwoven with another. **Vertical integration:** relates lower and higher levels.

### Five patterns of curriculum organisation

**1. Subject-centred** - the oldest and most used. Knowledge is placed in compartments called subjects with rigid boundaries. It goes back to ancient Greece and Rome's seven liberal arts: the **Trivium** (grammar, rhetoric, logic) and the **Quadrivium** (arithmetic, astronomy, geometry, music). Specialisation creates new subjects (biology led to botany and zoology).

- *Advantages:* a logical way to organise knowledge; teachers are trained in it; textbooks are written this way; evaluation and college entry are based on subjects; encourages specialisation.
- *Criticisms:* fragments knowledge; encourages rote learning; limits broad ideas and their application; encourages atomisation (more and more subjects); stresses facts for their own sake without social function.

**2. Organisation by correlation** - subjects keep their boundaries, but related content is presented side by side (fact to fact, or principle to principle), e.g. history with geography, mathematics with physics.

**3. Broad field (fusion)** - removes boundaries by combining areas into one large field, e.g. geography, civics and history become **social studies**.

- *Advantages:* comprehensive knowledge; removes excess detail; shows interrelatedness; flexible selection of content.
- *Criticisms:* superficial, survey-style treatment; heavy demands on teachers who must read widely; too many generalisations can discourage inquiry.

**4. Child-centred** - based on Rousseau, Froebel and Dewey. The child is at the centre of learning. Dewey: education is a process of living, not a preparation for future living. The curriculum is adapted to the learner's needs and interests, with play, observation, speaking, finding out, creating and projects. Two types: one stresses the learner's **interests**, the other the learner's **experience**.

- *Advantages:* learners solve problems; not limited by subject boundaries; active learners; real and related to the learner's culture; flexible, with participation in evaluation.
- *Criticisms:* learners have different and numerous interests; a child's interest may conflict with society's aspirations; some interests are unimportant or should be discouraged; hard to tell an abiding interest from a passing fad.

**5. Organisation based on persistent life situations (core curriculum)** - shifts attention from subject matter to social needs. Experiences are organised around problems learners meet repeatedly (political, social, economic). In Nigeria, themes could include sex education, social justice, economic reconstruction, poverty alleviation, peace education, environmental education, family planning and population explosion. Onwuka (1981): broad problems, units or unifying themes chosen because they allow effective teaching of the basic content of other subjects.

- *Advantages:* removes narrow compartments; learners take part in planning and so work harder; flexible; learners see the worth of what they learn.
- *Criticisms:* society changes so fast it may be impossible to implement; today's problems may not be the learner's future problems; difficult to evaluate because experiences change often.
