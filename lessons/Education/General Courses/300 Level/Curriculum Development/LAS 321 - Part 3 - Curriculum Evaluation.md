# LAS 321 Curriculum Development - Part 3

## Curriculum Evaluation

*Source: EMSA LASU 300 Level (2019/2020) compendium, cleaned up for Project-NODE.*

---

A good curriculum should include evaluation strategies. To make sound decisions, one must look at instructional methods, programmes, procedures and the goals they serve.

### Assessment, measurement and evaluation are not the same thing

- **Assessment** - finding out how students or groups have learnt a set of skills or knowledge, using measuring techniques, tests or examinations (Hudson, 1973). Assessment is part of evaluation.
- **Measurement** - determining the degree to which an individual has a certain attribute. It **quantifies** observed characteristics using set rules and criteria. (Measurement is status determination.)
- **Evaluation** - more than either. It is **worth determination**, and its main focus is helping people make better decisions.

### Definitions of evaluation

- **Tyler (1949):** determining to what extent the educational objectives are actually being realised by the programme of curriculum and instruction.
- **Scriven (1967):** a broad and continuous inquiry into the effects of educational content and process according to clearly defined goals.
- **Tawney (1976):** collecting and using information to make decisions about an educational programme.
- **Wheeler (1967):** not only determining outcomes and comparing them with expected outcomes, but also judging the nature and desirability of any change shown.

### What information is evaluated

1. **Antecedent** - the situation in which the curriculum is implemented: books, time available, facilities, other resources.
2. **Transaction** - the process of interaction during implementation (Stufflebeam, 1971 called this **process evaluation**).
3. **Outcome** - the pupil's change in behaviour (achievement and attitude) and the teacher's feelings about teaching. Four outcomes matter: achievement of students, attitudes of students, effects on teachers, effects on the community.

### Forms of evaluation

- **Formative evaluation** - assessing a programme while it can still be modified. A feedback arrangement that lets planners, designers and implementers improve the product. Stufflebeam: evaluation for **decision making** (proactive).
- **Summative evaluation** - evaluating the total curriculum plan; a summary conclusion on how far students reached the goals of a course or segment. Stufflebeam: evaluation for **accountability** (retroactive).

### Evaluating textbooks

Ask:

1. What category of students is the book meant for (target population)?
2. Is it recommended by examination bodies?
3. Is the print size and style of the text suitable for the target audience?
4. Is the book readily available?
5. Is it appropriate to the students' level?
6. Are pictures, graphs and charts clear and well labelled?
7. Are examples drawn from the learners' local environment?
8. Is the content relevant to the objectives of the subject?

### Steps in evaluating a curriculum project

1. **Identify the goals** of the project, stated operationally in clear, measurable terms.
2. **Collect data** that will help determine how effective the project is.
3. **Determine cost-effectiveness** - relate progress towards the goals to the resources spent so far.

### Characteristics of curriculum evaluation

1. Valuing
2. Goal oriented
3. Comprehensiveness
4. Diagnostic validity
5. Continuity
6. Diagnostic reliability
7. Integration of findings
8. Progress towards the goal

### Functions of evaluation

1. Shows those involved in planning or implementing what they have accomplished (level of success).
2. Helps clarify stated objectives.
3. Unanticipated learning results from the process.
4. Ensures accountability.
5. Helps in judging and deciding on teacher-student relationships.

### Instruments for evaluation

Mental ability (intelligence) tests, achievement tests, appreciation tests, aptitude tests, attitude inventories, checklists, diagnostic tests, case studies, interview schedules, observation schedules, personal inventories, rating scales, sociometric devices, vocational interest inventories, attitude scales, surveys.

The most commonly used are the **interview, questionnaire, scales and tests.**

- **Interview** - asking questions verbally to get information on feelings, beliefs, intentions, facts.
- **Questionnaire** - standard instructions and questions in written form, with written responses. Two types: structured (closed form) and unstructured (open form).
- **Scales** - numbers assigned to subjects or behaviour to quantify and measure qualities (Stufflebeam, 1971). Used for attitudes, values and interests.
- **Tests** - measuring instruments for certain characteristics of learners. Three main types:
  - **Aptitude test** - measures inborn or acquired characteristics. Used for job selection, admission, or assessing capability in some areas.
  - **Intelligence test** - measures general cognitive functioning: perceiving relationships, solving problems, applying knowledge. Result is the Intelligence Quotient: **IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) x 100**. Mainly used to identify mental retardation or gifted children.
  - **Achievement test** - measures mastery and proficiency; the dependent variable when evaluating a curriculum or method. Four main types:
    - **Essay test** - few questions that allow free response.
    - **Objective test** - many items; the testee supplies missing words or picks the correct answer (supply items, alternative response, multiple choice, matching).
    - **Performance test**
    - **Oral test**
