# Project Stakeholder Management

*Lecture handout (PMT 200 Level), converted for Project-NODE*

---

## Definition of stakeholders

Stakeholders are individuals, groups or organisations with an **interest in a project**. They can influence the project or be affected by its outcomes. Their involvement depends on their roles, responsibilities and interests.

They range from sponsors who provide funding, to customers who expect a product that meets their needs, regulatory bodies that enforce standards, and employees or suppliers affected by how the project is executed.

Effective stakeholder management aligns expectations with project goals, which reduces risk and builds support. Ignoring stakeholders can lead to resistance or even project failure. To manage them you must first **identify** them, then understand their **interests and influence**, then address their needs and concerns.

## Stakeholder mapping: the Power-Interest Grid

Stakeholder mapping identifies and prioritises stakeholders by their **influence (power)** and **interest**. The Power-Interest Grid has four groups:

| Group | Meaning | Approach | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High power, high interest** | Key players with a major impact | Manage closely; engage frequently | Project sponsors, senior executives |
| **High power, low interest** | Hold power but are not concerned with day-to-day activity | Keep satisfied; avoid unnecessary interaction | Senior managers not directly involved |
| **Low power, high interest** | Very interested but cannot make major decisions | Keep informed and engaged; their support matters | Team members, end users |
| **Low power, low interest** | Need minimal communication, but should not be neglected | Monitor | General public, external partners |

Mapping lets you spend communication effort where it matters most.

## RACI matrix

The RACI matrix clarifies **roles and responsibilities** so no role is overlooked. Each stakeholder is assigned one of four categories for each task:

- **Responsible (R):** does the work or completes the task.
- **Accountable (A):** ultimately answerable for the outcome; makes the final decision.
- **Consulted (C):** gives input and expertise before decisions are made.
- **Informed (I):** kept up to date on progress, but does not contribute to the task or decision.

Example (software project): the software engineer is **R** for coding; the project manager is **A** for delivery; the business analyst is **C** for requirements; the customer is **I** about progress.

Using a RACI matrix reduces confusion, clarifies ownership and improves efficiency.

## Types of stakeholders

**Internal stakeholders** are within the organisation:
- **Project sponsor** - funds the project and sets high-level objectives; ensures alignment with business goals.
- **Project manager** - plans, executes and communicates; manages timelines, resources and stakeholders.
- **Project team members** - carry out the tasks that produce deliverables.
- **Executives and senior management** - make strategic decisions and provide resources and guidance.

**External stakeholders** are outside the organisation but affected by or involved in the project:
- **Customers** - expect the project to meet their needs; their feedback drives requirements.
- **Regulatory bodies** - make sure the project complies with laws and standards.
- **Suppliers and vendors** - provide materials, services or expertise.
- **Local communities** - may be affected, especially by large infrastructure projects; their concerns shape public perception.

## Stakeholder analysis (four stages)

1. **Identify stakeholders** - list all individuals and groups with an interest (internal and external).
2. **Assess influence and interest** - rate each stakeholder's power and concern, to prioritise attention.
3. **Understand expectations** - find out what they expect, what worries them and how they can contribute.
4. **Develop engagement strategies** - customise how to involve each group.

Example: in a hospital construction project, patients, doctors, government agencies and contractors have different levels of interest and influence. Some care about the design, others about the budget or regulations.

## Stakeholder management (four stages)

1. **Identify stakeholders** - brainstorming, reviewing project documents, consulting experts.
2. **Plan stakeholder engagement** - match strategies to expectations, influence and interest (the Power-Interest Matrix).
3. **Manage stakeholder engagement** - carry out the plan: meetings, progress reports, collaboration tools.
4. **Control stakeholder engagement** - monitor interactions and feedback, address concerns, adjust the strategy.

## Stakeholders across the project life cycle

| Phase | Stakeholder focus |
|---|---|
| **Initiation** | Identify key stakeholders; analyse interests, influence and expectations |
| **Planning** | Develop engagement strategies; align communication with goals |
| **Execution** | Manage interactions; give regular updates; handle concerns |
| **Monitoring and control** | Evaluate satisfaction; adjust engagement |
| **Closure** | Record lessons learned; keep key relationships |

Example: in a product launch, marketing is most active during execution, while legal plays a key role during planning to ensure compliance.

## Stakeholder communication

Plan four things for each stakeholder group:

- **Audience** - who needs what information, in what format.
- **Mode** - meetings, reports, emails, etc.
- **Frequency** - how often (some need frequent updates, some periodic).
- **Method** - structured reports, informal discussion or presentations, to suit preferences.

Example: sponsors may prefer monthly executive reports; developers may need daily stand-ups.

## Reporting to stakeholders

- **Status reports** - progress, challenges and next steps.
- **Risk reports** - potential issues and mitigation plans.
- **Financial reports** - budget performance, expenses and resource allocation.

Example: a construction project manager may give weekly progress updates to site supervisors and quarterly financial reports to investors.
